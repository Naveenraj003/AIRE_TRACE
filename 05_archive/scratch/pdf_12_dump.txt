Abstract—The increasing deployment of autonomous tool-
using AI agents creates demand for mechanisms that provide
prospective warning of task failure from partial execution
trajectories, rather than retrospective diagnosis after failure
has occurred. Prior work on online agent-failure warning has
demonstrated that prefix-based failure prediction is viable, but
existing methods each use their own datasets, prefix definitions,
calibration procedures, and metrics, making comparative claims
difficult to interpret.This paper presents AIRE-Trace, a stan-
dardized prospective evaluation protocol for online agent-failure
warning that fixes information visibility, failure-target definition,
alert semantics, calibration procedure, false-alarm constraints,
and lead-time measurement. We further propose trajectory risk
decomposition (dyn_comp), which combines static and dynamic
risk signals from trajectory prefixes to improve prospective recall
under the protocol’s false-alarm budget.
The protocol is applied to representative heuristic (H1–H4),
classical machine-learning (M1), sequence-model (S1, S2), and
risk-decomposition (dyn_comp) warning methods on 636 recon-
structed episodes (a 19.18% strict-retention sample of the 3,316
declared source rows), split task-disjointly into TRAIN (163),
CALIBRATION (57), and TEST (416). Under the stringent
5% false-alarm rate (FAR) constraint, dyn_comp achieved a
modest prospective Recall@FAR of 0.1167 (FAR = 0.0404),
compared with the M1 baseline at 0.0078 (FAR = 0.0303). A
paired episode-cluster bootstrap (B = 10,000) confirmed the
difference (∆= +0.1089, 95% CI [0.0706, 0.1496], padj = 9.999×
10−5). Baseline AUROC ranged from 0.5074 to 0.6371. Fea-
ture ablation showed that omitting cumulative error and retry
signals yielded the largest observed drop in discrimination
(∆AUROC = −0.1069). Pairwise Direction Consistency across
seven baselines was 0.8294 (21/21 informative pairs), indicating
largely but not universally consistent rankings.
Index Terms—Agentic systems, failure warning, prospective
evaluation, trajectory analysis, standardized benchmarking, on-
line monitoring, calibration, bootstrap inference, risk decompo-
sition.
I. INTRODUCTION
The deployment of autonomous agentic systems—agents
that plan, invoke tools, and execute multi-step tasks with
limited human oversight—has raised reliability questions that
standard outcome benchmarking cannot fully answer. Bench-
marks such as AgentBench [1], AgentBoard [2], and GAIA [3]
measure whether an agent ultimately succeeds or fails, but
this final-outcome evaluation is insufficient for monitoring
purposes: by the time the outcome is observed, the agent has
already consumed resources and potentially caused unrecov-
erable side effects.
Prospective failure warning addresses a complementary
question: given only the portion of the trajectory observed
so far (a prefix), can a monitor produce a timely alert that
the episode will likely fail before the outcome is known?
This early-warning capability would enable intervention—
pausing the agent, escalating to human review, or switching
strategies—before failure becomes irreversible.
Recent work has shown that prefix-based failure prediction
is achievable. PrefixGuard [7] demonstrates prefix-based warn-
ing monitors on WebArena and TerminalBench. AgentFore-
sight [8] employs an RL-trained 7B-parameter auditor. Trajec-
tory Guard [9] applies real-time sequence anomaly detection.
However, these methods each use their own datasets, prefix
definitions, calibration procedures, and metrics. No existing
work cross-compares heuristic, classical-ML, and sequence-
model warning methods under one fixed protocol, making it
unclear whether reported advantages are robust to a common
evaluation protocol or partially artifacts of differing evaluation
conditions.

AIRE-Trace addresses this gap with two contributions:
1) A standardized prospective evaluation protocol for
online agent-failure warning—fixing information visi-
bility (pre-onset prefix only), failure-target definition,
alert semantics, calibration procedure (FAR ≤5%),
and statistical inference (episode-cluster bootstrap with
Holm–Bonferroni correction).
2) Trajectory risk decomposition (dyn_comp), which
combines static base-rate and dynamic trajectory-prefix
risk signals to improve prospective recall under the
protocol’s false-alarm constraint.
AIRE-Trace is an offline evaluator of pre-recorded agent
trajectories. It does not perform live intervention.
Research Question. Do comparative performance claims
for online agent-failure warning methods remain consistent
when methods are evaluated under identical information,
target, alert, calibration, lead-time, and compute constraints?
II. RELATED WORK AND COMPARATIVE GAP
A. Agent Outcome Benchmarking
Outcome benchmarks [1]–[3] establish performance base-
lines for agent capabilities but do not provide mechanisms for
prospective mid-execution monitoring. TRAIL [4] articulates
the need for robust evaluation of agentic traces, presenting 148
human-annotated traces for trace-debugging evaluation.
B. Retrospective Failure Attribution
Post-hoc attribution methods identify where or why a
trajectory went wrong after failure has occurred. Agen-
tRx [5] provides post-hoc localization of critical failure steps.
Who&When Pro [6] addresses retrospective attribution using
12,326 trajectories. AgentProcessBench [11] introduces step-
level quality evaluation with 8,509 human-labeled annotations.
These approaches require the complete trajectory and are
retrospective by construction.
C. Prospective Agent-Failure Warning
PrefixGuard [7] achieves AUPRC of 0.900 on WebArena
and 0.557 on TerminalBench under its own evaluation pro-
tocol. Direct numerical comparison with AIRE-Trace results
is not appropriate due to differences in prefix representation,
feature regime, and calibration protocol. AgentForesight [8]
employs an RL-trained auditor. Trajectory Guard [9] applies
sequence anomaly detection. Advani [10] characterizes false
success in LLM agents, showing that TF-IDF detectors achieve
AUROC 0.83–0.95 for false-success detection. These works
collectively establish prospective warning as a viable research
direction but operate in isolation without cross-comparison
under a common protocol.
D. Evaluation Methodology
Cameron, Gelbach, and Miller [12] establish the pairs
cluster bootstrap as the appropriate inference procedure when
observations are dependent within clusters. Holm [13] intro-
duced the sequentially rejective multiple test procedure for
familywise error rate control.

III. AIRE-TRACE METHODOLOGY
The AIRE-Trace prospective evaluation protocol enforces
strict separation between pre-onset trajectory monitoring, of-
fline threshold calibration, and test-set prospective evaluation,
as illustrated in Fig. 1.
A. Prospective Evaluation Protocol
An agent episode consists of a sequence of events
e1, e2, . . . , eT produced by an autonomous agent executing a
task. Each event contains role information (agent, tool, user,
system), action type, tool names, and error indicators. The
episode has an eventual outcome: eventual_success or
eventual_failure.
For TerminalBench episodes, the failure onset is defined as
tonset = min(incorrect_step_ids). Episodes lacking
this annotation are excluded from onset-dependent metrics
(Recall@FAR, lead time) but remain eligible for ranking
metrics (AUROC, AUPRC).
At checkpoint t
∈
{1, . . . , min(Ti, K)} with horizon
K = 30, a monitor accesses only the prefix (e1, . . . , et).
The checkpoint schedule is outcome-independent and episode-
symmetric. An alert at checkpoint t is prospective if and only
if t ≤tonset; an alert at t > tonset is a late detection and is
reported separately in the operational analysis. The prospective
evaluation semantics are illustrated in Fig. 2.

Evaluation Modes. Mode A (TerminalBench, primary):

B. Dataset and Task-Disjoint Splits
The primary corpus is Tracebench (3,316 declared source
rows). After provenance auditing and reconstruction, 2,655
rows were structurally invalid due to missing trajectory ar-
tifacts, 25 contained unreachable artifact paths, and network
recovery attempts yielded zero additional valid episodes,
leaving 636 canonical episodes (19.18%) for benchmarking.
These exclusions represent strict structural data-availability
attrition; however, missingness cannot be assumed to be miss-
ing completely at random (MCAR). Consequently, systematic
selection bias cannot be ruled out, and all empirical findings
remain formally bounded to this audited 636-episode cohort.
The overall pipeline structure is depicted in Fig. 1.
The 636 episodes are split task-disjointly (entire episode to
exactly one split): TRAIN (163), CALIBRATION (57), TEST
(416). The TEST population contains 317 failed episodes (257
with valid onset annotations, 60 without), and 99 successful
episodes.
C. Threshold Calibration and Alert Semantics
The calibration procedure selects τ ∗= arg maxτ Recall(τ)
subject to FARcalib(τ) ≤0.05, on the CALIBRATION split
only. Ties are broken by lower FAR first, then higher threshold.
The selected threshold τ ∗is frozen before TEST evaluation.
D. Trajectory Risk Decomposition
The trajectory risk decomposition method (dyn_comp)
decomposes the failure risk score into an empirical static task-
difficulty prior and a dynamic prefix-derived risk signal in (2):
sdyn comp(t) = PM1(fail | e1:t) −Pstatic(fail | task)
(2)
where PM1(fail | e1:t) is the dynamic failure probability
predicted by the logistic baseline M1 from the 13 prefix
features x1:t, and Pstatic(fail | task) is a static prior fitted
on the TRAIN split from task category and difficulty failure
base rates. Both components are strictly computable at prefix
checkpoint t without future trajectory leakage. The residual
score sdyn comp(t) ∈[−1, 1] isolates excess dynamic risk
relative to static task expectations. The operating threshold
τ ∗= 0.5522 is calibrated on CALIBRATION under the
FAR ≤5% constraint, identical to all baselines.

Standard baseline comparisons use B = 1,000 iterations. The
primary dyn_comp vs. M1 comparison uses B = 10,000
iterations. Episodes are resampled with replacement at the
episode level (not prefix level) to preserve within-episode de-

independence and producing artificially narrow CIs.

α = 0.05.

A. Primary Prospective Warning Results
Table II presents the primary Mode A TEST performance.
Under the FAR ≤5% constraint, dyn_comp achieved Re-

0.5522). The M1 baseline achieved Recall@FAR of 0.0078

In Table II, dyn_comp’s protocol-consistent episode-
balanced prefix AUROC is 0.3593. Because dyn_comp sub-

failure-ranking AUROC is 0.6940), early non-erroneous pre-

scores, producing a sub-0.5 global prefix AUROC. The resid-

global risk score; the primary evaluation remains thresholded
prospective warning. At the frozen operating threshold (τ ∗=
0.5522), dyn_comp fires only on extreme positive dynamic
risk spikes, achieving Recall@FAR of 0.1167 at FAR of
0.0404, while the M1 baseline yields Recall@FAR of 0.0078
at FAR of 0.0303.

E. Operational Analysis
The operational analysis examines false alarms, late detec-
tions, and alert burden under frozen thresholds, summarized in

episodes and 19.84% of valid failure episodes received only

represents retrospective diagnosis, not prospective warning.
Among models operating within the FAR target, S1 pro-

among all evaluated methods.
VI. DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS
A. The Operational Recall Collapse
The most critical finding is the collapse of prospective
Recall@FAR under the 5% FAR constraint: the baselines with
a defined Recall@FAR achieved zero or near-zero prospective
recall; H3 was undefined because the 5% FAR constraint
was not achievable. S1’s AUROC of 0.6371 demonstrates
meaningful probabilistic discrimination, but the discriminative
signal is insufficient to generate threshold-based alerts on in-
dividual failure episodes before onset without FAR violations.
dyn_comp’s Recall@FAR of 0.1167 represents a statistically
significant improvement over M1’s 0.0078, but the absolute
recall remains modest. The gap between discriminative ranking
capability and operational early-warning utility is a structural
finding of this evaluation.
B. Selective Statistical Significance
The bootstrap-tested AUROC advantages for S1 are statis-
tically significant only against S2, H2, and H3. Its advan-
tages over M1, H1, and H4 are not statistically significant
after Holm–Bonferroni correction. The empirical ordering
S1 > M1 > H1 > H4 by point estimate does not reflect
universally distinguishable statistical differences.
C. Limitations
Population coverage and selection bias. The 636 re-
tained episodes represent 19.18% of the 3,316 declared source
rows. Because exclusion was driven by artifact corruption
and unreachable paths rather than random subsampling, the
retained cohort may systematically differ from the excluded
episodes across task complexity, agent run length, or failure
distribution. The reported failure distributions and warning
behaviors are valid within the audited canonical population,
but generalization to the uncurated source population remains
unverified.
Incomplete onset annotation. 60 of 317 TEST failure
episodes (18.9%) lack onset annotations. These episodes
are excluded from onset-dependent Recall@FAR and late-
detection analysis but remain available for ranking metrics.
Systematic differences between annotated and unannotated
episodes may affect reported prospective-warning rates.
LLM-monitor evaluation. The L (LLM-monitor) model
was not evaluated because the available hardware (RTX 2050,
4 GB VRAM) could not support protocol-compliant evaluation
over the full dataset. No conclusions about L’s prospective
performance can be drawn.
Domain scope. Transferability of the protocol and results
is restricted to the evaluated tool-use coding-task domain.
Generalization to other agent types or task domains has not
been established.
Offline evaluation. AIRE-Trace is an offline evaluator of
recorded trajectories, not a runtime intervention mechanism.
Deployment considerations are outside the scope of this work.
